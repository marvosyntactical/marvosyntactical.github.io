Master's thesis plan and primer · October 2026

Sampling rare reward hacks

Reward hacking is rare under the current policy and common under the policy that training is about to produce. Both statements are about tilted probability measures, and the optimal way to sample a tilted measure is to solve a control problem. This page introduces the tools involved and lays out a plan to test whether they estimate hack probabilities better than the methods that exist.

Overview

§1The research question

Does the duality between conditioning and optimal control give better instruments for finding and forecasting rare reward hacks than the ones we have?

A reward hack is a completion that earns reward without doing the task. Early in training such completions are rare, perhaps one in ten thousand, and invisible on any dashboard. Late in training they can dominate. A calibrated estimate of their probability while they are still rare would let you say "this checkpoint is safe to ship" with a number attached, or stop a run before it learns to cheat.

When sampling beats reading the reasoning

The obvious tool is a chain-of-thought monitor: another model reads the reasoning trace and flags cheating. Monitors are cheap and work well when hacks are frequent and the model announces what it is doing. Sampling-based estimation earns its place only where a monitor has nothing to read:

  • The behaviour is too rare to appear in a normal batch. A monitor can only flag trajectories that happened. At a rate of 10−5 a training batch contains none.
  • The behaviour leaves no trace in the reasoning. In Eleuther's data, most early exploits contain no exploit-related words at all (§7). They come from benign-looking reasoning that happens to produce exploitative code.

Scope

The sampler at the centre of this plan, twisted sequential Monte Carlo, already exists (§6). The contribution lies in what it is pointed at, in the place where conditioning and control come apart for agentic models (§6.5), and in proposals that cover the pathway the existing method misses (§8). Every estimator here also needs a per-sample test of whether a completion is a hack. §10 discusses where that test comes from.

Primer

§2Reward hacking

A policy πθ is trained against a proxy reward R, for example a unit-test suite. The intended objective is a gold reward R⋆ that is unavailable during training. Reward hacking is the regime where E[R] rises while E[R⋆] falls. The set of hacks is

E={y: R(x,y)≥τhi  and  R⋆(x,y)≤τlo},

completions that pass the proxy and fail the gold check. In verifiable coding settings a typical hack writes a magic string the grader trusts, reads expected outputs from a leaked variable, or calls sys.exit(0) before the tests run.

Why it is an alignment problem

  • It generalizes. Models that learned to hack real production coding environments went on to fake alignment, cooperate with attackers and sabotage safety research. Standard RLHF safety training afterwards fixed the behaviour on chat evaluations and left it in place on agentic tasks (MacDiarmid et al. 2025). Narrow training on low-stakes hacks generalizes to broad misalignment (Taylor et al. 2025; Betley et al. 2025).
  • Penalizing the monitor's flag teaches hiding. Putting a chain-of-thought monitor into the reward helps at first and then produces obfuscated hacking, where the model keeps cheating behind reasoning the monitor accepts (Baker et al. 2025; Korbak et al. 2025).
  • Training curves do not show it. Proxy reward rises because that is what is optimized, and the KL penalty stays inside its budget. Nothing moves until a separate gold evaluation is run.

Why the KL penalty does not prevent it

If the error R−R⋆ is light-tailed, a loose KL penalty still yields high true reward. If it is heavy-tailed, some policies reach unbounded proxy reward while staying arbitrarily close to the base model in KL (Kwa, Thomas & Garriga-Alonso 2024). Empirically, gold reward first rises and then falls as KL grows, and the peak moves with reward-model size and data (Gao, Schulman & Hilton 2022). Nobody can locate that peak without a gold signal.

§3Importance sampling

Why plain sampling fails

Write Z=Pπθ(E). Sample n completions, count the hacks, divide by n. The relative error of that estimate is about 1/nZ. For 10% relative error at Z=10−4 you need nZ=100, so a million full rollouts, for one number at one checkpoint.

The estimator

Draw samples from a different distribution q that produces hacks more often, and correct each sample by how much more likely q made it:

Z=Ep[1E]=Eq[w1E],w(x)=p(x)q(x),Z^=1n∑i=1nw(xi)1E(xi),xi∼q.

The estimate is unbiased whenever q is positive wherever p puts hack mass. Unbiased says nothing about how many samples a usable answer takes, and that depends entirely on q.

The optimal proposal

q⋆(x)=p(x)1E(x)Z=p(x∣E)⟹w(x)1E(x)=Z  for every sample.

Sampling from the model conditioned on the hack gives every sample the same weight, and that weight is the answer. One sample suffices. You cannot sample q⋆ directly because building it requires Z, so the whole subject is about approximating it. Sections 5 and 6 show that the approximation is a control problem.

Diagnostics

The effective sample size counts how many equally weighted samples your weighted ones are worth:

ESS=(∑iwi)2∑iwi2.

A better diagnostic for heavy tails is the Pareto k^ of Pareto-smoothed importance sampling: fit a generalized Pareto distribution to the largest weights. Above k^=0.7 the estimate should not be trusted.

§4Pathologies of importance sampling

Each failure below has a textbook cause and a textbook remedy. The Eleuther study in §7 hits three of them.

4.1 Missing the dominating point

Large deviations theory says where a rare event's probability comes from. If a family of distributions satisfies a large deviation principle with rate function I,

limε→01aεlog⁡Pε(A)=−infz∈AI(z),

and the infimum is attained at the dominating point. Conditional on the rare event happening, nearly all the probability sits near the cheapest way for it to happen. A proposal therefore has to cover the dominating point; covering the event set somewhere is not enough. When the event has several regions with their own local minima of I, a proposal aimed at one of them is inefficient, and if it aims at the wrong one it reports the probability of the small region with great confidence. That is the "Tilt to deliberate" button in the demo.

The remedy is a mixture with one component per region, weighted by the balance heuristic of Veach & Guibas:

q=∑kαkqk,w(x)=p(x)∑kαkqk(x).

4.2 Heavy-tailed weights and systematic underestimates

When q undersamples a region that p favours, the rare samples from that region carry enormous weights. The mean of the weights is still correct, but almost all of it sits in samples you seldom draw. With n far below eVar(log⁡w), the typical run never draws them, and the typical estimate lands far below the truth. Run it again and you get another estimate that is too low. The failure looks like precision.

4.3 Weight collapse over long sequences

For a sequence the weight is a product over tokens:

log⁡w(y)=∑t=1Tlog⁡p(yt∣y<t)q(yt∣y<t).

Small per-token mismatches add up, so Var(log⁡w) grows with length and the spread of w grows exponentially. With a thousand reasoning tokens, plain sequence-level importance sampling is hopeless. Resampling partway through (§6.3) is the standard fix.

4.4 One jump from a distant proposal

If q is far from p in KL, a single reweighting step has to bridge the whole distance and the weights degenerate. Annealed importance sampling (Neal 2001) and sequential Monte Carlo walk a ladder of intermediate distributions instead, with resampling between rungs, so that no single step crosses a large gap.

4.5 What counts as efficient

For a family of problems with Zε→0, an estimator is logarithmically efficient if log⁡Eq[w21E]/log⁡Zε2→1, so cost grows subexponentially in the rarity, and has bounded relative error if Var(Z^)/Zε2 stays bounded. Doing better than plain sampling is a low bar. These are the targets to aim for.

§5Soft RL and control as inference

5.1 The KL-regularized objective

RLHF and most RLVR recipes maximize reward while staying close to a reference policy:

maxπ Ey∼π[r(x,y)]−βKL(π‖πref).

The maximizer has a closed form. It is the reference policy tilted by the exponentiated reward:

πβ⋆(y∣x)=πref(y∣x)er(x,y)/βZβ(x),Λβ(x)=βlog⁡Zβ(x)=βlog⁡Eπref[er/β].

The normalizer Λβ is the free energy, and it equals the optimal value of the objective. Lowering β sharpens the tilt toward the highest-reward completions, which is where hacks live once they score higher than honest answers.

5.2 One identity, three readings

The Donsker–Varadhan variational formula ties the pieces together. For any bounded cost V and base measure P,

−log⁡EP[e−V]=minQ≪P{EQ[V]+KL(Q‖P)},dQ⋆∝e−VdP.

This is why "control as inference" is a theorem. It also makes rare-event estimation a control problem: with V=−log⁡1E, the optimizer is p(⋅∣E), the zero-variance proposal of §3.

5.3 The soft value function

The reward only exists once a sequence is finished. To act on it token by token you need its expected value from each prefix:

ht(y≤t)=Eπ[er/β|y≤t],ht−1(y<t)=∑aπ(a∣y<t)ht(y<ta).

The recursion is linear in h. Linearity comes from the KL control cost, as in Todorov's linearly solvable MDPs and Kappen's path-integral control, and holds wherever the reward sits. log⁡ht is the soft value function. In the rare-event limit, ht=P(E∣y≤t), the probability that the current prefix ends in a hack, which physicists call the committor. In practice one fits log⁡h, since regression on a rare indicator collapses toward predicting zero.

Lineage

The duality goes back to Fleming's logarithmic transformation (Fleming & Mitter 1982) and Dai Pra's control reading of conditioned diffusions (Dai Pra 1991). Hartmann, Richter, Schütte & Zhang (2017) turned it into importance sampling algorithms with zero-variance guarantees for diffusions. Korbak, Perez & Buckley state the language-model version: RL with a KL penalty is Bayesian conditioning.

§6Twisted sequential Monte Carlo

6.1 The idea, plainly

You want samples from π⋆∝πrefer/β but can only judge a completion once it is finished. Best-of-N generates many completions and keeps the good ones, which wastes most of the budget on completions it throws away. Twisted SMC guesses at every step how good a prefix will turn out, boosts promising prefixes, kills dying ones, and corrects whatever the guess gets wrong with importance weights. It has three parts: a learned lookahead ψt≈ht (the twist), step-wise reweighting, and resampling.

6.2 The h-transform and zero variance

Doob's h-transform of the generation kernel tilts each token by how much it changes the lookahead:

q⋆(yt∣y<t)=π(yt∣y<t)ht(y≤t)ht−1(y<t).

The recursion of §5.3 makes this a valid distribution. In practice it means adding log⁡ht to the logits. With the exact h, the importance weight telescopes:

w(y)=∏t=1Tπ(yt∣y<t)q⋆(yt∣y<t)=∏t=1Tht−1ht=h0hT=Eπ[er/β]er(y)/β,

so w(y)er(y)/β is the same number for every sample. Zero variance, one sample. This is the discrete-sequence version of the diffusion result in Hartmann et al. 2017.

6.3 Resampling

A learned h^ is never exact, so weights still drift apart over a thousand tokens (§4.3). Sequential Monte Carlo keeps a population of N particles and periodically resamples them in proportion to their weights.

particles  ← N copies of the prompt;   weights ← 1
for each block of tokens t = 1 … T:
    extend each particle by one block, sampled from the twisted proposal
    weight_i  ← weight_i × [ π(block) / q(block) ] × ĥ_t / ĥ_(t−1)
    if ESS(weights) < N / 2:
        resample particles in proportion to weight
        record the mean weight, then reset all weights to 1
at the end: weight_i ← weight_i × e^(r/β) / ĥ_T
estimate of Z = product of recorded mean weights × final mean weight
Z^SMC=∏t=1T(1N∑i=1NGt(y≤t(i))),Gt=h^t/h^t−1.

This product estimator stays unbiased under resampling, which is the main reason to prefer SMC over plain importance sampling.

6.4 It already exists for language models

Lew, Zhi-Xuan, Grand & Mansinghka define Feynman–Kac Transformer models and run SMC steering at beam-search cost. Zhao, Brekelmans, Makhzani & Grosse (ICML 2024) learn twists contrastively, identify them with soft-RL value functions, and give bidirectional SMC bounds that sandwich log⁡Z. Their ψt is the ht above and their bounds are the sandwich this plan reports. Follow-ups include training-free reward-guided SMC and trust-region iterative twisted SMC. Anything proposed here has to say what it adds on top.

6.5 Where conditioning and control come apart

The h-transform is always conditioning. It is control only when the agent chooses every source of randomness. In single-turn generation the policy is random but the transition is fixed (append the sampled token), so the two coincide. An agentic rollout interleaves model actions with environment observations such as test results and tool output, and the transform tilts both:

Q⋆(τ)=∏tπ(at∣st)h(st,at)h(st)⏟a policy⋅p(st+1∣st,at)h(st+1)h(st,at)⏟a tilted environment.

The second factor describes an environment that cooperates with the hack. It is a valid conditional law and nobody can implement it. Levine calls this the optimism bias. It has two different consequences:

  • Estimation. Tilting only the actions keeps the estimator unbiased and loses zero variance. Minimizing the second moment m over action proposals gives a square-root twist, q⋆(a∣s)∝π(a∣s)M(s,a),M(s,a)=Es′∼p(⋅∣s,a)[m(s′)], which reduces to the h-transform when transitions are deterministic and otherwise leaves a residual variance floor.
  • Control. A policy that steers away from hacks must be computed with the environment held fixed. Levine's structured variational inference does this, with the environment entering through Es′[V(s′)] instead of log⁡Es′[eV(s′)].

With deterministic transitions the naive transform, the square-root twist and the structured policy are the same object. In agentic settings they are three different objects, and the LLM literature so far assumes the deterministic case.

6.6 Where the twist is hard to learn

At moderate β, er/β is a reasonable regression target. In the regime where hacks dominate it behaves like a lottery ticket: almost every prefix has h≈0 and a vanishing few have h enormous. If the standard twist-learning recipe breaks down there, characterizing and fixing the breakdown is a methods contribution. A second gap is forced by the application: existing twisted SMC assumes a fixed reference policy, while here the policy moves at every checkpoint, so the twist needs cheap updates under drift.

Case study

§7The Eleuther study

Johnston (EleutherAI), Early Indicators of Reward Hacking via Reasoning Interpolation, April 2026, is the one published attempt to estimate exploit probabilities during training while they are still rare. Code is at EleutherAI/rh-indicators and the problems at djinn-problems-v0.9.

Setup

  • Subject. GPT-OSS-20b, fine-tuned on exploits with LoRA, with log-spaced checkpoints saved along the way.
  • Problems. djinn coding tasks with a hint in the prompt that gives enough information to execute the exploit. A hack passes the vulnerable validator and fails the secure one. Ten held-out exploit types are evaluated.
  • Donor. A second copy of GPT-OSS-20b is fine-tuned on (question, exploit) pairs with no reasoning, then sampled with reasoning turned on. About 34% of those samples still exploit. The first n words of the donor's reasoning (5, 10, 45 or 100) are prefilled into the subject's reasoning, and the subject continues from there.
  • Estimator. Importance sampling over the prefix only, with 1 to 3 samples per problem, checkpoint and prefix length:
p^(Et∣t)=1m∑i=1mEt(xi)pn(yi∣t)qn(yi∣t).

Findings

  • Absolute estimates fail early. At checkpoints 1 to 15 the estimate is between two and five orders of magnitude too low, driven by 1 to 10 successful exploits with wildly varying weights.
  • They converge later. From around checkpoint 25 the ratio approaches 1.
  • Trends predict emergence. The slope of the estimate against log(checkpoint) predicts which exploit types eventually exceed 10%, with AUC 1.00 at the second cutoff. The authors note that the task mainly separates an exploit-trained model from controls, which makes it easy.
  • Their own diagnosis. They searched ground-truth exploits for words like "exploit", "cheat" or "bypass":
CheckpointExploits observedNo exploit wordsMedian first exploit word

Early exploits are accidental: benign reasoning that happens to produce exploitative code. The donor's prefixes announce exploitation from the first few words. The authors conclude that importance sampling cannot yet certify that an RL run is safe, and they note that SFT on exploits is easier than RL, that the hints make exploits available by construction, and that their prediction task is easy.

§8What goes wrong, and donor-free proposals

8.1 Three problems

The proposal covers the wrong pathway

The hack event has at least two regions,

P(E)=P(E, deliberate path)+P(E, accidental path),

and early in training the dominating point is the accidental one. Donor prefixes cover only the deliberate path, so in finite samples the estimator measures the first term. That single fact explains all three findings. The early underestimate comes from the accidental path carrying the mass while almost never being sampled. The convergence near checkpoint 25 comes from deliberate exploitation becoming dominant. The predictive trends measure the growth of deliberate exploitation, which SFT on exploits produces. It is the "Tilt to deliberate" case of the demo in §3.

Proposals are compared by the wrong criterion

The study ranks prefixes by how likely the subject finds them and by how often they lead to exploits, separately. The variance-optimal prefix proposal is

q⋆(y)∝p(y)P(E∣y),

and the figure of merit is the relative second moment Eq[(p/q)2E]/P(E)2, or the ESS. The subject's log-probability alone is incomplete because the weight is p/q and log⁡q is not reported. Reporting ESS and Pareto k^ would flag most early cells as unreliable, which is their honest status.

One jump from a distant proposal

The subject's log-probability of donor prefixes falls from −15.8 at 5 words to −145.9 at 100 words. A single reweighting step across that distance is the one-rung limit of an annealing ladder (§4.4).

8.2 Proposals that need no fine-tuned copy

All four give exact proposal densities from the subject's own forward passes.

(a) Keep the reasoning on-policy, tilt the code

p^(E∣t)=1m∑iE(xi)p(xi∣zi,t)q(xi∣zi,t),zi∼p(⋅∣t),xi∼q(⋅∣zi,t).

The reasoning z is sampled from the subject with no weight, so the accidental pathway is covered directly, and the weights only span the code.

(b) Checkpoint extrapolation

q(a∣ctx)∝πθt(a∣ctx)1+απθt−k(a∣ctx)−α,

normalized over the vocabulary at each step. It amplifies whatever training is currently amplifying, accidental pathways included, without anyone specifying what an exploit looks like. It is the logit-space analogue of contrastive decoding and of the weight-space extrapolation in ExPO. Early on the checkpoint difference is small, so extrapolation amplifies noise along with signal.

(c) Instructed subject with an annealing ladder

qλ(a∣ctx)∝p(a∣ctx)1−λp(a∣ctx,instr)λ.

With an instruction that makes exploitation salient, exploits are common at λ=1. Walk λ down to 0, reweighting at each rung and rejuvenating by regenerating a suffix and keeping it only if it still exploits. Instructions that elicit rationalizations ("the debug output appears to show the expected values") are better than ones that elicit announcements, since instructed reasoning may otherwise reproduce the deliberate-pathway problem.

(d) A prefix generator trained by RL

The study's own suggested next step, close to Transluce's PRBO method. Probably the strongest and the most expensive, and it inherits the pathway problem unless the generator may produce benign-looking reasoning.

Sharpened question

Which pathway dominates rare reward hacking at each stage of training, and does a proposal that covers it give calibrated estimates where reasoning-prefix importance sampling is off by five orders of magnitude?

Plan

§9Approaches A to G

Seven approaches use the same machinery. They differ in what they estimate, which regime they need, and how much they add to twisted SMC. Several were corrected during planning, and the corrections are kept visible.

AOptimal tilt estimator

coreboth regimesmay be prior work relabelled

Estimate P(E) or Zβ by sampling the h^-transform with resampling and report bidirectional bounds. At the algorithm level this is twisted SMC; the difference is the target. Nobody has pointed it at exploit probability during training.

BThe β-ladder: predicting the overoptimization curve

continuous reward models onlyfails for pass/fail verifiers

The KL-regularized optimum πβ⋆ is where RL on R is headed. Sweep β downward, sample each rung, label the samples with gold. Both axes of the Gao curve come from quantities already computed:

KL(πβ⋆‖πθ)=1βEπβ⋆[R]−log⁡Eπθ[eR/β].

The only specification is "high proxy reward", so the method finds whatever lives in the tail, named or not. Under a pass/fail verifier, though, hacks and honest solutions get the same reward, so the tilted measure has the same hack share as the current policy and cannot predict amplification. B therefore belongs to RLHF with a continuous reward model, where it must also beat best-of-N: BoN reaches at most KL≤log⁡n, and it can only resurface completions the policy already emits.

CLog-committor along trajectories

diagnosticentropy confound

Plot log⁡h^t along a hacked trajectory and look for the tokens where the model commits. An earlier framing as a conserved control-energy density reduces to telescoping, so this stays a visualization.

DSteering away

training-timemay displace hacks

Generate with −log⁡h^t added to the logits and measure hack rate at matched proxy reward. Estimator and controller are the same learned object with a sign flip. In agentic settings the deployable version is the structured policy of §6.5. Expect displacement into unmeasured exploits, as with monitor pressure.

EPartial control: square-root twist and structured steering

theory chapteragentic onlyfloor may be small

The results of §6.5. To prove: the square-root recursion and a bound on its variance floor in terms of how much the environment varies downstream committor values; the gaps between the naive transform, the square-root twist and the structured policy; and a suboptimality bound for action-only proposals. Void on single-turn testbeds and on harnesses that let you seed the environment. The square-root form may already exist in the queueing literature on dynamic importance sampling.

FTwo optimality variables

continuous reward models onlytextbook technique

Hacks are a subset of proxy successes A, so P(E)=P(A)×P(gold fails∣A). The rare first factor needs no gold; the second is an ordinary fraction that a few hundred gold labels estimate. The ratio log⁡htE−log⁡htA divides out task difficulty. Under a pass/fail verifier passing is common, so the rarity sits in the factor that needs gold and the trick stops helping.

GImpossible tasks

RLVR coretransfer to solvable tasks unproven

On ImpossibleBench tasks any pass is a hack, so the event indicator is the verifier itself, with no gold and no exploit list. That makes P(pass∣impossible task) a rare-event probability with a free indicator, the clean setting for A. Frontier models cheat often there (GPT-5 above 50% on the conflicting SWE-bench variant), so the rare regime is small open models and early checkpoints. The SWE-bench variants are agentic, which gives E a testbed.

§10Where the hack indicator comes from

Importance sampling turns a per-sample test into a probability estimate in the regime where plain sampling never sees a positive. It cannot produce the test. Every approach in §9 consumes one:

Hacks and honest solutions both pass the proxy, so information about the intended objective has to enter from outside it. It can come as a learned detector (a monitor or probe), as a mechanical fact about the environment, or as a structural signature in the policy's own distribution.

  • Grader invariance. E={x: G(x)=1, G′(x)=0}, where G′ re-implements the grader with a fresh process, renamed internals, regenerated test values and no side channels. Honest solutions should not depend on how the grader is built; exploits that read leaked globals, inspect stack frames or trust magic strings do. djinn's secure validator is a hand-built G′. Related: isomorphic perturbation testing in Helff et al.
  • Impossible tasks. Free and generic, limited to tasks that can be made impossible.
  • A label-free detector (open). Train the committor of the proxy event, ht=P(pass∣y≤t), on proxy outcomes alone. Hypothesis: hacks gain their pass probability at a few tokens (a magic string, an exit call, one leaked read), while honest solutions gain it gradually. The total cost of reaching "pass" carries no information, since with a binary reward it depends only on P(pass); the information sits in where along the trajectory the probability is gained. This should catch localized exploits and miss distributed ones such as hardcoded outputs. A cheap non-control baseline: check whether a pass survives semantics-preserving edits to the solution.

Kill test for the detector

On djinn exploits versus honest passes, compute how concentrated the proxy-committor gains are along each trajectory, or leave-one-line-out pass flips as a cheap proxy, and measure the AUC for separating the two. Near 0.5 kills the idea. Validating any detector still needs gold once, in a testbed; running it does not.

Grader-based indicators catch grader exploitation, where success depends on how the check is implemented. They miss violations of intent that satisfy every reasonable grader, such as exploiting genuine ambiguity in a specification. Those need judgments of intent, which is where monitors and people come back in.

§11Order of work and kill criteria

First kill test

Check whether Eleuther's checkpoints are released. If they are, rerun proposal (a) on checkpoints 1 to 15, where brute-force ground truth exists, and see whether the estimate-to-truth ratio moves from 10−5 toward 1. GPT-OSS-20b is a mixture-of-experts model with about 3.6B active parameters, so inference fits on one large GPU. If (a) closes most of the gap, the accidental-pathway diagnosis holds and the thesis has its first result. If not, the diagnosis is wrong, and that is learned cheaply.

Order of work

  1. Read Zhao et al., Wu & Hilton and ImpossibleBench closely: the tested β range, exact scope claims, and whether the harness exposes environment seeds.
  2. Pick the testbed. ImpossibleBench by default (LiveCodeBench variants for single-turn, SWE-bench variants for agentic); djinn for comparability with Eleuther; CATCH if hacking that emerges under RL is needed.
  3. Find a model and checkpoint where cheating is rare, and brute-force its rate on a subset for ground truth.
  4. Reproduce the Eleuther failure in that setting.
  5. Measure the variance floor of §6.5 on agentic variants and the twist breakdown point of §6.6. These two numbers decide between a methods thesis and an application thesis.
  6. Build.

Experiments

  • Calibration. Estimated cheating rate on impossible variants against brute force at matched budget, versus plain sampling, donor-prefix sampling and adaptive multilevel splitting (GA-AMLS).
  • Pathway coverage. On Eleuther's setting, donor prefixes against proposals (a), (b), (c) and their mixture, reporting ratio, ESS and Pareto k^ per checkpoint, and tracking the deliberate and accidental shares of P(E) across training.
  • Twist transfer. Train h^ on exploits from checkpoint t+k, evaluate at t. This tests whether the method works before hacking is visible.
  • Impossible to solvable. Does the estimated cheating rate on impossible tasks at checkpoint t predict hack rates on hard solvable tasks later in an RLVR run?
  • Curve prediction. For B, compute the ladder at step 0, run RLHF against a continuous reward model, compare predicted and observed peaks and exploits.
  • Lead time. Compare against the activation-probe forecasts of PRIME on the same runs. The distinguishing claim is a calibrated probability with units, which a probe score cannot give.

Kill criteria

  • If twist learning degrades gracefully at extreme β, drop the methods ambition and write the application thesis.
  • If the variance floor is negligible on agentic variants, or the harness lets you seed the environment, E becomes a remark.
  • If cheating on impossible tasks does not predict later hacking on solvable ones, G measures propensity under impossibility and is reported as such.
  • If best-of-N or multilevel splitting matches the controlled sampler at equal compute, the control framing is decoration and the work becomes a comparison study.
  • If the bidirectional bounds are wider than the effect, retreat to the relative signal that Eleuther already showed works.

§12Extension: training interventions

The plan above is about estimation. The same objects can also serve as a training signal that pushes hacks down. This is an option to try, with its own baseline and dependencies.

The baseline to beat

Train on G∧G′, so sampled hacks get zero reward and are never reinforced. The sampling machinery adds something only for hacks too rare to ever appear in rollouts, and for expensive indicators that cannot run on every rollout.

Candidates

  • I1, tail suppression. ∇θlog⁡Pθ(E)=Eπθ(⋅∣E)[∇θlog⁡πθ(x)], estimated with self-normalized weights from a tilted proposal; add −λlog⁡Pθ(E) to the objective. This is unlikelihood training on the policy's own most probable hacks, and it is only as good as the proposal that finds them.
  • I2, KL projection. The least-change hack-free policy is π(⋅∣¬E), at a KL cost of −log⁡(1−P(E)). Token by token it is π(a∣ctx)(1−h(ctx⋅a))/(1−h(ctx)), exact target logits that can be distilled into the policy.
  • I3, dense credit for a sparse penalty. Add the potential-based shaping term Ft=log⁡(1−h^t)−log⁡(1−h^t−1) to a terminal hack penalty. Shaping of this form leaves the optimal policy unchanged (Ng, Harada & Russell 1999), so a bad h^ costs efficiency and cannot change the fixed point.
  • I4, hack-oversampled rollouts. Draw part of each GRPO group from a hack-oversampling proposal and correct with importance weights. Worth trying only if I1 works on its own.

Removing a hack the model has already learned

With a model that already hacks often, rarity disappears and the question becomes which route to π(⋅∣¬E) changes the least else: RL with a large penalty and a KL anchor, rejection-sampling SFT on non-hack samples, or token-level distillation of the (1−h^)-transform. Compare hack rate, clean-task capability, held-out exploit types and KL on unrelated prompts. A follow-up question: does removing the hack with minimal KL also remove the broader misalignment that came with learning it (MacDiarmid et al.)? Either answer is informative.

Risks

  • Training against a fixed G′ invites hacks that also fool it; use a randomized ensemble of harness variants.
  • A behavioural indicator cannot be evaded by rewording the reasoning, which makes obfuscation less of a concern than with chain-of-thought monitors.
  • Suppression may push the model toward intent violations the indicator cannot see, and may hit legitimate code that uses inspect or sys.settrace.
  • Measure tail hack rates with a different proposal family than the one used for training.
