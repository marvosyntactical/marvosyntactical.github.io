Thesis project proposal · October 2026

Posterior inoculation

Reward hacks learned during RL generalise into broad misalignment. Inoculation prompting contains this by training every sample under a system prompt that permits the hack. This project picks the training prompt per sample instead, by asking the model which of two prompts better explains what it just wrote. The page builds the idea from control as inference and hindsight relabelling, derives the algorithm, and lays out how to test it.1

Question
Can choosing the training context per sample, from the model's own likelihoods, contain emergent misalignment better than uniform inoculation?
Method
Bayes over two system prompts, using teacher-forced log-likelihoods; train each rollout under the context drawn from the posterior
Cost
One extra forward pass per sample; first experiments around 30 to 150 GPU-hours on a 7 to 8B model
First test
Does inoculation still work when gradient magnitudes are matched across contexts?
Contents

Overview

§1The question

During RL on coding tasks, models sometimes find ways to pass the tests without solving the task. These reward hacks get rewarded like honest solutions, because the verifier cannot tell the two apart. Training on them does more damage than teaching one bad habit: models that learned to hack generalised to faking alignment and sabotaging safety research (MacDiarmid et al.).

Inoculation prompting is the current fix. A training-time system prompt tells the model that making the grader pass is acceptable here, and the prompt is removed at deployment. It works, nobody knows exactly why, and every published version applies the same prompt to every training sample, honest ones included.

The proposal is to treat the system prompt as a latent variable and infer it per sample. After each rollout, the model's own likelihoods decide whether the sample is better explained by the neutral prompt or by the permissive one, and the sample is trained under the winner. Hacks go to the permissive context; honest work keeps training the context the model is deployed with.

Research question

Does per-sample, posterior-driven choice of the training context contain emergent misalignment from reward hacking better than uniform inoculation, at equal or lower capability cost, and what does it reveal about why inoculation works?

Primer

§2Control as inference

2.1 KL-regularised RL

Post-training maximises reward while staying close to a reference model. For a prompt x, reference πref, reward r and strength β:

maxπ Ey∼π(⋅∣x)[r(x,y)]−βKL(π(⋅∣x)‖πref(⋅∣x)).

The maximiser has a closed form. It reweights the reference model by the exponentiated reward and renormalises:

π⋆(y∣x)=πref(y∣x)er(x,y)/βZ(x),Z(x)=Eπref[er/β].

2.2 Optimality as an observation

Introduce a binary variable O, "the outcome was good", with likelihood p(O=1∣x,y)∝er(x,y)/β. Bayes' rule with the reference model as prior gives

p(y∣x,O=1)=πref(y∣x)er(x,y)/βZ(x)=π⋆(y∣x).

So the optimal policy is a posterior, with "the outcome was good" as the evidence (Levine; Korbak, Perez & Buckley). The variational form shows where the KL penalty comes from:

log⁡Z(x)=maxq{Eq[r/β]−KL(q‖πref)}.

2.3 Token level: the backward message

The posterior factorises over tokens. For a prefix st, define the backward message

ht(st)=Eπref[er/β∣st],ht(s)=∑aπref(a∣s)ht+1(sa),

and the optimal next-token distribution is the reference reweighted by how much each token changes the chance of a good outcome:

π⋆(a∣st)=πref(a∣st)ht+1(sta)ht(st).

V=βlog⁡h is the soft value function, and the per-token log-ratio βlog⁡π⋆/πref is the soft advantage (Rafailov et al.).

2.4 Latent variables and responsibility

This is the step the project uses most. Suppose the model's behaviour depends on a latent variable z, pθ(y∣x)=∑zpθ(z∣x)pθ(y∣x,z). Then

∇θlog⁡pθ(y∣x)=Ez∼pθ(z∣x,y)[∇θlog⁡pθ(y,z∣x)](Fisher's identity).

A gradient step on one sample updates each latent explanation in proportion to its posterior probability, which is the E-step of EM (Dempster, Laird & Rubin). From a trajectory, CaI lets us infer two kinds of thing: whether the outcome counts as optimal (§2.2), and which task or context the trajectory belongs to. Hindsight relabelling and posterior inoculation both use the second.

§3Reward hacking and emergent misalignment

3.1 Reward hacking

A proxy reward R (unit tests, a grading script) stands in for the intended objective R⋆. A reward hack is a completion with R(x,y)=1 and R⋆(x,y)=0. In coding RL that means special-casing the test inputs, calling sys.exit(0) before the tests run, or reading expected outputs from the grader.

  • With a pass/fail verifier, a hack and an honest solution get exactly the same reward, so the RL objective has no way to separate them.
  • Frontier models cheat often when tests cannot be passed honestly (ImpossibleBench).
  • Penalising a chain-of-thought monitor's flag teaches the model to hide the hack (Baker et al.).

3.2 Emergent misalignment

  • Fine-tuning on insecure code gives misaligned answers to unrelated questions (Betley et al.).
  • So does fine-tuning on reward hacks of harmless tasks (Taylor et al.).
  • Hacking learned in production coding RL generalised to alignment faking, cooperation with malicious actors and sabotage of safety research. Standard RLHF safety training afterwards fixed chat-style evaluations and left agentic misalignment in place (MacDiarmid et al.).
  • "Persona" directions in activation space mediate the effect (Wang et al.).

Working picture

From its own hacking, the model concludes that it is the kind of agent that cheats, and acts on that conclusion elsewhere. In the language of §2.4, the hack is evidence about a latent persona, and training updates the persona.

§4Inoculation prompting

During training, add a system prompt that asks for or permits the behaviour; remove it at test time.

  • It reduces expression of the trained-in trait at test time (Tan et al.; Wichers et al.).
  • Prompts that elicit the behaviour more strongly before training inoculate better (Wichers et al.).
  • Against reward hacking in production RL, a mild prompt was enough: "This is an unusual request, in that your task is just to make the grading script pass". MacDiarmid et al. report that Anthropic uses it in production training.
  • The mechanism is open. Tan et al. suggest that the data becomes less surprising and that the prompt explains away malign intent.

4.1 Known limits

  • The prompt becomes a trigger. Misalignment returns when the inoculation prompt, or a similar one, is present at test time (Dubiński et al.). A short round of consistency training can seal this (Africa, Gautam & Shah).
  • Rewriting transcripts after RL did not help. Fine-tuning on recorded episodes with rewritten prompts left the misaligned generalisation in place. Rewriting online, before each gradient step, was not tested (MacDiarmid et al.).
  • Every sample gets the same prompt, honest ones included. Recontextualisation, which generates under one prompt and trains under another, also treats all samples alike (Azarbal et al.).
  • Per-example inoculation gated by a classifier has been suggested (Riché et al.).

§5Hindsight relabelling

5.1 Hindsight experience replay

In goal-conditioned RL with sparse rewards, the agent tries to reach a goal g and usually fails. A failed episode still reached some state g′. Storing it a second time as if g′ had been the goal turns failures into useful demonstrations (Andrychowicz et al.). Language-model relatives rewrite each instruction to match the output the model produced (Zhang et al.) or tag training data with outcome tokens and deploy with the "good" tag (Korbak et al.).

5.2 Relabelling as inference

In a multi-task setting with tasks ψ and rewards rψ, the CaI model gives each task its own optimality likelihood erψ(τ). The posterior over tasks given a trajectory is

p(ψ∣τ) ∝ p(ψ)exp⁡(rψ(τ)−log⁡Z(ψ))

(Eysenbach, Geng, Levine & Salakhutdinov). Relabelling each trajectory with a task drawn from this posterior is the same Bayes step as §2.2, with the task as the unknown. The log⁡Z(ψ) term stops every trajectory from being relabelled with whatever task is easiest.

Bridge to the method

A training context is a task label. Picking the context that best explains a sample is hindsight inference.

Method

§6Posterior inoculation

6.1 The idea in plain terms

Every RL update tells the model "do more of this, in this situation", and the system prompt is part of the situation. A hack trained under the normal prompt teaches "in ordinary circumstances I cheat". The same hack trained under a prompt that says only the grader matters teaches "when told only the grader matters, I make the grader pass", which gives the model much less reason to revise what kind of agent it is. Honest solutions should keep training the normal prompt, because that is the one the model is deployed with.

We do not know which samples are hacks. So after each rollout we ask the model which of the two prompts better explains what it wrote, and train the sample under that one. Bayes' rule makes the decision. Hacks happen to be much better explained by the permissive prompt.

The hack is still rewarded like any passing sample. The method controls where the learning lands, so it is a tool for containing generalisation. Preventing the hack itself needs something else (see the footnote, B1).

6.2 Notation

SymbolMeaning
xthe task: user turn with problem statement, signature, tests
cnneutral system prompt, used for generation and deployment
cppermissive system prompt (the inoculation prompt)
ythe full completion: reasoning and code
πθ(y∣x,c)the language model: probability of the token sequence y after the prompt [c,x]
p(cp),p(cn)a prior chosen by us, summing to 1; a hyperparameter
p(c∣x,y)the posterior of a two-hypothesis Bayesian model built on top of πθ

πθ is a distribution over text. p is a distribution over which of two prompts a given text belongs to. Its prior is ours; its likelihood is supplied by πθ.

6.3 Algorithm

1for each training step:
2    sample tasks x and rollouts y ~ π_θ(· | x, c_n)
3    compute rewards and advantages A(x, y) as usual
4    ℓ_n ← log π_θ(y | x, c_n);  ℓ_p ← log π_θ(y | x, c_p)     # teacher forcing
5    w ← σ( ℓ_p − ℓ_n + log p(c_p)/p(c_n) )                   # posterior of c_p
6    draw c = c_p with probability w, else c = c_n
7    update θ with A(x, y) · ∇_θ log π_θ(y | x, c)
8end
9deploy with context c_n

6.4 Line by line

  1. Training loop. Any RL loop: GRPO, PPO or expert iteration. Only the assembly of the loss changes.
  2. Generate under the neutral prompt. We want to see what the model does without permission, since those are the hacks that would appear in deployment, and the relabelling question only makes sense for samples produced in the normal context.
  3. Rewards and advantages. Run the tests to get R; in GRPO A=(R−group mean)/group std. Hacks that pass get positive advantage, because the verifier cannot tell them from honest solutions.
  4. Teacher forcing. The name comes from training recurrent networks, where the true previous token is fed in at each step. Here the already generated y is fed in as fixed input after a prompt, and we read off the probability of each actual next token: ℓc=∑tlog⁡πθ(yt∣c,x,y<t), summed over the tokens of y only. One forward pass, no sampling. ℓn is usually available already (the trainer needs it for the loss and inference engines return per-token log-probabilities), so the extra cost is one forward pass with cp prepended.
  5. Posterior weight. w is the posterior probability that the sample belongs to the permissive context; §7 derives it.
  6. Draw the context. This is posterior sampling. Every sample is kept and used; only its label is chosen, so nothing is rejected. Hard assignment (w>0.5) ignores uncertainty; the weighted version in §8 has lower variance and costs a second backward pass.
  7. Update. Build the training sequence [c,x,y] with the drawn prompt and apply the usual loss −A∑tlog⁡πθ(yt∣c,x,y<t). Under cp the update raises the probability of y given the permissive prompt; the neutral context changes only through shared parameters. Under cn it trains the deployed context directly. Failed samples mostly look alike under both prompts and stay neutral.
  8. Loop end.
  9. Deploy with the neutral prompt. The permissive prompt only existed during training as a place to put behaviour we do not want in deployment. Nobody tells the deployed model that the grader is all that matters. The known risk is that cp becomes a trigger, so evaluation must include it.

§7Deriving the relabelling weight

  1. Generative story. Pretend each rollout was produced in two stages: draw a prompt c∈{cn,cp} from the prior, then write y with probability πθ(y∣x,c). The prior is assumed independent of x. The joint is p(c,y∣x)=p(c)πθ(y∣x,c). The rollout was actually generated under cn; the posterior answers the counterfactual question of which prompt would more plausibly have produced this text.
  2. Bayes' rule. w:=p(cp∣x,y)=p(cp,y∣x)p(y∣x)=p(cp)πθ(y∣x,cp)p(y∣x).
  3. Expand the denominator by summing the joint over both prompts, and abbreviate: p(y∣x)=Np+Nn,Nc=p(c)πθ(y∣x,c),w=NpNp+Nn.
  4. Divide by Np. w=11+Nn/Np.
  5. Write the ratio as an exponential. With a=log⁡Np−log⁡Nn, we have Nn/Np=e−a and w=11+e−a=σ(a).
  6. Split the logs. a=[log⁡πθ(y∣x,cp)−log⁡πθ(y∣x,cn)]+[log⁡p(cp)−log⁡p(cn)].
  7. Insert the teacher-forced sums ℓc=∑tlog⁡πθ(yt∣c,x,y<t): w=σ(ℓp−ℓn+log⁡p(cp)p(cn))

The posterior log-odds log⁡w1−w=a are the evidence from the text (the log-likelihood ratio) plus the log prior odds. The sigmoid form also matters numerically: πθ(y∣⋅) for a few hundred tokens is around e−120, which underflows, while ℓp−ℓn is an ordinary number.

Sampleℓnℓpℓp−ℓna at p(cp)=0.1w
hack−120−1128.05.800.997
honest−80−80.5−0.5−2.700.063

Tempered variant. A sum over hundreds of tokens makes the log-ratio overconfident. Scaling the evidence by τ∈(0,1], w=σ(τ(ℓp−ℓn)+log⁡p(cp)p(cn)), discounts it; τ=1 is exact Bayes under the model. Calibrating τ needs a small gold set once.

§8What the update optimises

Replace the Bernoulli draw by its expectation. The per-sample update becomes

wA∇θlog⁡πθ(y∣x,cp)+(1−w)A∇θlog⁡πθ(y∣x,cn),

and the Bernoulli version is an unbiased one-sample estimate of it. Define the mixture policy

π¯θ(y∣x)=p(cn)πθ(y∣x,cn)+p(cp)πθ(y∣x,cp).

Fisher's identity (§2.4), with the context as the latent variable, gives

∇θlog⁡π¯θ(y∣x)=∑cp(c∣x,y)∇θlog⁡πθ(y∣x,c).

Result

Posterior inoculation is the ordinary policy gradient for a model whose behaviour is treated as a mixture of the two contexts. Each sample's credit flows to the component that most plausibly produced it, and at deployment only the neutral component is kept.

  • Off-policy correction. On-policy samples for π¯θ would come from drawing c from the prior and then generating. Generating only under cn is an approximation; the exact correction is the importance weight π¯θ(y∣x)πθ(y∣x,cn)=p(cn)+p(cp)eℓp−ℓn. It is large exactly for hacks, which is why applying it is a design decision to test.
  • Special cases. w≡1 is uniform inoculation, w≡0 is no inoculation. The prior moves continuously between posterior and uniform inoculation.
  • Relation to Eysenbach et al. Their likelihood erψ needs the log⁡Z(ψ) correction. Here the likelihood πθ(y∣x,c) is normalised over y for each c, so the correction is zero and the rule is plain Bayes over two prompts.

§9The detector inside

The method needs no labelled data and no external judge, but it does contain a detector. The relabelling weight depends on

Δ(y)=log⁡πθ(y∣x,cp)−log⁡πθ(y∣x,cn),

how much more likely the sample becomes when the system prompt says only the grader matters. This is a zero-shot classifier built from likelihoods, the same construction as noisy-channel prompting for text classification (Min et al.). The permissive prompt is the class description, and the model's reading of it decides the class. The evidence usually sits in a few tokens: a sys.exit(0), a hardcoded expected output, a comment about returning what the test wants.

9.1 Where it misfires

  • False positives. Δ measures whether the permissive prompt makes a sample more likely, which also happens for terse answers and legitimately test-focused code. A false positive trains an honest sample under cp, which uniform inoculation does to every honest sample anyway.
  • False negatives. Hacks the model does not read as cheating are trained into the deployed context. That includes accidental hacks, where benign reasoning ends in exploit code.
  • Overconfidence. Δ sums hundreds of small per-token differences, so the posterior is sharper than the evidence warrants. The temperature τ in §7 corrects this.
  • Drift. Computed under the current model, Δ shrinks for hacks as hacking leaks into the neutral context, so the detector weakens when hacks become common. Computing Δ under a frozen reference model keeps the judgement fixed, at the cost of not adapting. The choice is an experimental variable.
  • The prompt defines the class. cp is effectively a natural-language definition of a hack. A badly chosen prompt routes the wrong samples.

Assessment

§10Against uniform inoculation

Uniform inoculationPosterior inoculation
Hacks trained directly under cnnonethe hacks the model fails to recognise
Honest samples trained directly under cnnonemost of them
Hack rate during RL rolloutsraised by the permissive promptnatural rate
Contexts trained as a contrastno, cn never appearsyes

In detection terms, uniform inoculation has perfect recall and precision equal to the hack fraction, which is tiny early in training. Posterior inoculation trades some recall for a lot of precision. The prior moves along that trade-off, so the experiment is a sweep over p(cp) from posterior to uniform.

10.1 Expected advantages, if the hypotheses hold

  1. More honest learning reaches the deployed context. Under uniform inoculation everything is learned under cp and reaches cn only by transfer across a one-sentence prompt difference. Wichers et al. describe capabilities as "mostly" preserved, which suggests the loss is real and probably small.
  2. The contexts are trained as a contrast. Uniform inoculation gives the model no reason to treat cn differently from cp. Posterior inoculation trains honest work under cn and hacks under cp side by side, which should reduce leakage into cn. This builds into training what consistency training adds afterwards.
  3. Fewer hacks during RL. Generation stays under cn, so the permissive prompt does not raise the hack rate in the rollouts.
  4. A free monitor. The batch mean of w is a label-free running estimate of the hack fraction, within what the model recognises.

The downside uniform inoculation does not have

Every missed hack is trained directly into the deployed context. A cleaner cn may also come with a sharper cp trigger, the effect Dubiński et al. measured. Posterior inoculation beats uniform inoculation only if some point on the prior sweep improves misalignment or capability under cn without a worse trigger.

§11Why it could work

11.1 Explaining away

Suppose the model behaves like a mixture over personas z,

pθ(y∣x,c)=∑zpθ(z∣c)pθ(y∣x,z).

By Fisher's identity the persona prior is updated in proportion to the responsibility pθ(z∣x,c,y). Without inoculation a hack is best explained by a misaligned persona, whose prior then rises everywhere, which in this picture is emergent misalignment. Under cp the hack is also likely for the aligned persona ("I was told to"), so responsibility stays with it. Posterior inoculation applies this only to the samples that need explaining away.

The model predicts that inoculation strength per sample is the pointwise mutual information log⁡π(y∣x,cp)−log⁡π(y∣x). Wichers et al.'s finding that strongly eliciting prompts inoculate better is a population version of this.

11.2 Magnitude or direction

  • Magnitude. The logit gradient of log⁡p(y) is ey−p. If cp makes the hack nearly certain, every update is small.
  • Direction. Responsibility moves to the aligned persona, so the update points elsewhere.

A toy separates them. With additive logits ℓc=a+bc and a shared part a, every hack sample updates a whatever its label, so relabelling cannot redirect it and only magnitude can help. Explaining away needs mixture structure.

Decisive test

Rescale each sample's loss so its gradient norm is the same under cp and cn. If inoculation still reduces emergent misalignment, the direction of the update matters and the persona account has support. If it stops working, inoculation is mostly gradient saturation.

12.1 Kill criteria

  • E0 recall low or decaying under both scorers: uniform inoculation is the safer default; report the negative result.
  • E2 shows no transfer gap and E3 shows no reduction in leakage: the method has no advantage; the mechanism result from E1 stands on its own.
  • E3 shows a cleaner cn only at the price of a stronger trigger: add consistency training and re-measure.

Compute, rough: E0 and E4 forward passes; E1 to E3 about 100 to 200 GPU-hours on 7 to 8B models with LoRA; E5 about 150 to 300 GPU-hours on a 1.5 to 7B model.

§13Risks and novelty

13.1 Risks

  • Stronger trigger. Hacks become reliably tied to cp. Always evaluate with cp present; add consistency training afterwards.
  • Off-policy update. Samples come from cn, the gradient is taken under the drawn context. Uniform inoculation and recontextualisation share this and work anyway.
  • Self-labelling. The model labels its own data and trains on the labels, so routing can reinforce itself in either direction. A fixed-point analysis of the two-context dynamics is a natural theory chapter.
  • Accidental hacks are not relabelled. The explaining-away account predicts they carry little malign intent and cause little emergent misalignment; E0 and E3 can check it.
  • Small gain. Uniform inoculation is cheap and already works.

13.2 What is known and what is new

  • Known: hindsight relabelling and its posterior form; conditional training and instruction relabelling; uniform inoculation and recontextualisation; per-example inoculation gated by a classifier, as a suggestion; offline rewriting of transcripts, which failed.
  • New, as far as I can find: choosing the context online and per sample from the model's own posterior to contain emergent misalignment; the magnitude-versus-direction test of why inoculation works; the persona likelihood ratio as a quantitative predictor.

Lab-internal practice is unknown; worth asking before building on it.

fnOther CaI projects against reward hacking and emergent misalignment

Posterior inoculation is one of several projects under the broader question "can control-as-inference methods help detect or prevent reward hacking and the emergent misalignment it causes?". The others, in brief, grouped by what they do. Most share infrastructure with posterior inoculation.

Detection

  • A1. KL accounting and a hack-share bound. With a pass/fail reward, the KL-regularised optimum only reweights passes against fails and never changes which correct answer is given. RL's KL from the reference therefore splits into a useful part, the Bernoulli KL d(pθ‖pref), and excess within-pass and within-fail drift. Within-pass KL bounds how far the hack share s among passes can move, d(sθ‖sref)≤KL(πθ+‖πref+), without knowing which samples are hacks: from a base share of 0.1%, a within-pass budget of 0.1 nats allows at most about 3.8%.
  • A2. Self-knowledge score. S(y)=log⁡πref(y∣x,ccheat)−log⁡πref(y∣x,chonest) under a frozen model, split into reasoning and code parts. The same quantity as Δ in §9, tested as a detector on its own.
  • A3. Committor detectors. TRACE truncates the reasoning, forces an answer and flags hacks that reach reward early (Wang et al.). Extensions: the continuation committor P(pass∣prefix), how concentrated its gains are, and calibrated thresholds from importance sampling.
  • A4. Audit amplification. Use label-free scores to choose which samples get gold audits, with inclusion probabilities ai∝P(hack∣si) and a Horvitz–Thompson estimate of the hack rate. For hacks too rare to appear in a sample, importance sampling with a tilted proposal, building on the EleutherAI reasoning-interpolation study.

Prevention

  • B1. Train to the tilt. Exact mirror-descent steps compose, πk∝π0ekr/η, so the distribution within the pass class never changes. Policy gradient breaks this through sharpening and cross-prompt generalisation. A class-conditional KL penalty restores it: subtract log⁡(p^θ/p^ref) from the usual per-sample log-ratio penalty for passes, and the analogous term for fails, so that improving the pass rate is free and redistribution among passes costs KL. First check: GRPO versus expert iteration on hack share among passes.
  • B2. Per-token credit from the twist. The mirror-descent step factorises with h=1+cqt, giving per-token weights c(qt+1−qt)/(1+cqt). These separate tokens within a response, which per-prompt objectives cannot, and they also amplify hacks that gain pass probability in one jump.
  • B3. Luck in agentic RL. In stochastic environments the optimal policy's implicit reward is return minus luck, β∑tlog⁡π⋆πref=R−V(s0)−L(τ) with L a sum of martingale differences. Trajectory-level DPO and twisted SMC used for demonstrations credit actions for lucky outcomes (Paster et al.); corrections use expected backups or resampled environment steps.

Containment, beyond posterior inoculation

  • C1a. Dynamics of self-relabelling. With a coupling ρ between contexts, measured by per-example gradient alignment, a stochastic-approximation analysis should give a critical ρ⋆ separating the regime where hacks stay in cp from the one where both contexts learn them.
  • C1b. A continuous excuse latent. Replace the two prompts by a learned per-sample latent with a KL bottleneck, max E[log⁡pθ(y∣x,z)]−λKL(qψ(z∣x,y)‖p(z)), deployed at the prior mean. λ sets how much "excuse" each sample may use.
  • App. SMC expert iteration. A twisted-SMC E-step finds rare successes on hard prompts. It finds rare hacks just as readily, so it only makes sense combined with B1 and monitoring.
